Predicting three-dimensional grain growth means connecting local grain-boundary motion to long-time changes in grain size, topology, and morphology. Physics-based simulations can generate the large datasets that machine-learning surrogates need, but a surrogate inherits the numerical artifacts of its training source along with the dynamics it was meant to learn. This dissertation develops a source-aware local-learning framework that moves from physically controlled stochastic simulations to direct learning from sparse four-dimensional experiments.

Two completed studies establish the simulation foundation. The first shows that the geometry and stochastic sampling of a local simulation neighborhood control lattice pinning and inclination-dependent anisotropy: Gaussian sampling suppresses strong lattice-aligned preferences, deliberately reshaped neighborhoods prescribe graded anisotropy, and rotating the neighborhood rotates the measured inclination response. What a training label contains is thus partly a property of the neighborhood that generated it, although these results do not establish universal material fidelity~\cite{tian2026neighborhood}.

The second study, 3D-PRIMME, shows that a local neural operator can learn controlled mode-filter evolution from minimal temporal supervision. The model reproduces major kinetic and topological trends, learns prescribed inclination dependence, and transfers without retraining from \(100^3\)-voxel training volumes to domains as large as \(1024^3\). That performance measures fidelity to the simulation teacher; it is not proof of universal grain-growth physics~\cite{tian2026threeDprimme}.

Ongoing work extends the same architecture to a five-state laboratory diffraction-contrast-tomography dataset. One field of view is selected through a residual-driven search, registered without label interpolation, linked across states, and used for direct training on the T0--T1 transition. Stable results show substantial suppression of artificial drift after curation. Evaluation on T2--T4, which were held out from fitting but have already been examined in the ongoing study, shows encouraging early coarsening and normalized size distributions together with unresolved late-time kinetics, topology, and individual-grain magnitude~\cite{tian2026experiment}.

The dissertation research has two experiment-centered aims organized by scientific dependency rather than completion date. Aim 1 establishes and evaluates direct experimental learning by combining the substantially completed single-window selection, label-preserving curation, linkage, and T0--T1 training workflow with retrospective held-out-from-training evaluation on T2--T4. Persistence, majority-class, unchanged-distribution, readout, and topology-sensitivity comparisons determine what the learned rollout adds beyond simple baselines; effective time remains a support-limited diagnostic, never a physical-time calibration. Aim 2 asks which spatial information the reliable experiment-trained model uses and how that information becomes a local evolution decision. Input-side scale attribution and output-side response and Jacobian analysis are the probes currently developed. The resulting claims remain bounded to the selected trajectory, but the workflow provides a source-aware basis for deciding which measured responses support model interpretation or limited physical attribution.
