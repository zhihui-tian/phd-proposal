Predicting three-dimensional grain growth means connecting local grain-boundary motion to long-time changes in grain size, topology, and morphology. Physics-based simulations can generate the large datasets that machine-learning surrogates need, but a surrogate inherits the numerical artifacts of its training source along with the dynamics it was meant to learn. This dissertation develops a source-aware local-learning framework that moves from physically controlled stochastic simulations to direct learning from sparse four-dimensional experiments.

Two completed studies establish the simulation foundation. The first shows that the geometry and stochastic sampling of a local simulation neighborhood control lattice pinning and inclination-dependent anisotropy: Gaussian sampling suppresses strong lattice-aligned preferences, deliberately reshaped neighborhoods prescribe graded anisotropy, and rotating the neighborhood rotates the measured inclination response. What a training label contains is thus partly a property of the neighborhood that generated it, although these results do not establish universal material fidelity~\cite{tian2026neighborhood}.

The second study, 3D-PRIMME, shows that a local neural operator can learn controlled mode-filter evolution from minimal temporal supervision. The model reproduces major kinetic and topological trends, learns prescribed inclination dependence, and transfers without retraining from \(100^3\)-voxel training volumes to domains as large as \(1024^3\). That performance measures fidelity to the simulation teacher; it is not proof of universal grain-growth physics~\cite{tian2026threeDprimme}.

Ongoing work extends the same architecture to a five-state laboratory diffraction-contrast-tomography dataset. Stable preliminary results demonstrate residual-driven window selection, integer registration without label interpolation, grain linkage, direct training from one experimental interval, replicate rollouts, held-out evaluation, and substantial suppression of artificial drift. Early coarsening and normalized grain-size distributions are reproduced. Late-time slowdown, topology, and some individual-grain responses remain unresolved~\cite{tian2026experiment}.

The proposed research has three aims. Aim 1 will establish experimental Level 1--2 reliability across grouped windows of the available volume, covering spatial reproducibility, empirical variability, null comparisons, and readout sensitivity. Aim 2 converts those frozen definitions into a single confirmatory test: experiment-trained rollouts will be evaluated once on a prespecified experimental partition used for neither training nor model selection, and each metric family will be adjudicated against its frozen null baseline as a pass, partial pass, or fail. Effective-time analysis will remain an explicitly support-limited diagnostic, never a physical-time calibration. Aim 3 addresses interpretability and physics learning: which spatial information does a validated experiment-trained model use, and how does it convert that information into local evolution decisions? Input-side scale attribution and output-side response and Jacobian analysis are the probes currently developed, but the aim is defined by that question, not by a fixed method set. The result will be a defensible account of when a local learned operator can be trusted relative to its data source, and a basis for physical interpretation of measured mesoscale evolution.
