Predicting three-dimensional grain growth means connecting local grain-boundary motion to long-time changes in grain size, size distribution, topology, and morphology. A supervised surrogate learns whatever its labels contain: from simulation, the simulator's discretization and stochastic policy; from experiment, acquisition motion, segmentation error, and identity-linkage failure mistaken for boundary motion. This dissertation develops a source-aware local-learning framework that moves from physically controlled stochastic simulations to direct learning from sparse four-dimensional experiments.

Two completed simulation studies and one substantially completed experimental workflow establish the foundation. The first shows that the geometry and stochastic sampling of a local simulation neighborhood control lattice pinning and inclination-dependent anisotropy: Gaussian sampling suppresses lattice-aligned preferences, reshaped neighborhoods prescribe graded anisotropy, and rotating the neighborhood rotates the measured response. It also identifies the mode filter as the only lattice-free teacher cheap enough for three-dimensional data generation~\cite{tian2026neighborhood}. The second, 3D-PRIMME, learns that mode-filter evolution from minimal temporal supervision, reproduces major kinetic and topological trends, recovers a prescribed inclination dependence, and transfers without retraining from \(100^3\)-voxel training volumes to \(1024^3\) domains~\cite{tian2026threeDprimme}. The third selects one field of view from a five-state laboratory diffraction-contrast-tomography dataset by a residual-driven search, registers and links it without label interpolation, and trains directly on the T0--T1 transition, reducing learned rollout drift by more than an order of magnitude~\cite{tian2026experiment}. Fidelity to a simulation teacher is not material fidelity.

The proposed research has two experiment-centered aims, organized by scientific dependency rather than completion date and governed by one evidence ladder separating field integrity, predictive fidelity, model interpretation, and physical attribution. Aim 1 evaluates the experiment-trained operator on T2--T4, which were held out from fitting but have already been examined: early coarsening and normalized size distributions are reproduced, while late-time kinetics, topology, and individual-grain magnitude are not. Its remaining work is the persistence, majority-class, unchanged-distribution, readout, and topology-sensitivity comparisons that determine what the rollout adds beyond simple baselines. Aim 2 asks which spatial scales an operator passing those gates uses, how that information becomes a local evolution decision, and which responses relate to measurable boundary geometry; input-side scale attribution and output-side Jacobian probes are developed and symmetry audited in simulation. The resulting claims remain bounded to the selected trajectory.
