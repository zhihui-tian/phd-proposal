\chapter{Introduction and Research Vision}

\section{Mesoscale prediction as a local-to-global problem}

The mechanical response of a polycrystal depends strongly on its grain size, and that size is set by grain growth during high-temperature processing~\cite{hall1951deformation,petch1953cleavage,hillert1965growth}. Grain growth is expressed macroscopically through changes in grain size, size distribution, topology, and morphology, but these observables emerge from local motion and rearrangement near grain boundaries. Predictive modeling has to bridge two scales: the local neighborhood in which an interface update occurs, and the large spatial and temporal domain over which those updates accumulate into measurable microstructural evolution. Established Monte Carlo, phase-field, and geometric approaches expose complementary kinetic, topological, and three-dimensional aspects of this problem~\cite{mullins1956motion,hillert1965growth,anderson1984kinetics,srolovitz1984topology,chen2002phasefield,krill2002phasefield,macpherson2007vonneumann,lazar2011threeD}. Direct three-dimensional simulation over experimentally relevant volumes and long time horizons remains computationally demanding, motivating learned surrogates that can reproduce local updates more efficiently~\cite{montes2021surrogate,yan2022primme,qin2024graingnn,tian2026threeDprimme}.

Machine learning does not remove the need to define physical content. A supervised model learns the relationships contained in its labels. When the labels come from simulation, the learned operator is limited by the simulator's assumptions, discretization, stochastic policy, and numerical artifacts. When the labels come from experiment, the model may also learn acquisition motion, reconstruction and segmentation error, and identity-linkage failures as if they were physical boundary motion. Fitting voxel transitions is the easy part of this problem. The harder question is whether the whole path from data source to local operator to long rollout can be trusted physically and statistically.

\section{Position relative to existing approaches}

Physics-based grain-growth models and learned surrogates answer different parts of the local-to-global problem. Monte Carlo and mode-filter methods provide efficient stochastic evolution with explicit control over the local update policy; phase-field methods resolve diffuse-interface evolution from prescribed free-energy and kinetic models; and geometric or topological descriptions expose relations among boundary motion, grain size, and connectivity~\cite{anderson1984kinetics,chen2002phasefield,krill2002phasefield,macpherson2007vonneumann,melville2024modefilter}. These approaches remain essential reference models, but applying them directly to a sparse sequence of categorical experimental grain maps still requires assumptions about constitutive parameters, temporal calibration, boundary conditions, and measurement uncertainty.

Learned surrogates for physical dynamics have converged on a small number of representational choices, and the distinctions among them decide what a model can be asked to do after training. Neural operators learn a mapping between function spaces and are therefore discretization invariant by construction, whether parameterized in Fourier space or through branch and trunk networks~\cite{li2021fno,lu2021deeponet,wang2021pideeponet}. Physics-informed formulations instead encode the governing equations in the loss, which presumes those equations are known~\cite{raissi2019pinn}. Graph-network simulators carry state on particles or mesh nodes and pass messages along edges, which suits adaptive discretizations and irregular geometry~\cite{sanchez2020gns,pfaff2021meshgraphnets,brandstetter2022mppde}, and hybrid learned-numerical solvers replace only the expensive term of a classical scheme~\cite{kochkov2021cfd}. Grain growth has now been approached from most of these directions: a Fourier neural operator gives resolution-invariant two-dimensional prediction~\cite{peivaste2025fno}, convolutional recurrent and graph models learn microstructure evolution with adaptive spatiotemporal resolution~\cite{yang2021convlstm,fan2024gnn}, and combined convolutional and graph networks have been used to scale kinetic Monte Carlo grain growth~\cite{tian2026scaling}.

Within that landscape, the approaches closest to this dissertation span phase-field-trained surrogates, local physics-regularized update models, dynamic graph models for three-dimensional microstructures, and high-fidelity deep-learning accelerators~\cite{montes2021surrogate,yan2022primme,qin2024graingnn,tep2025grain}. Their representations, supervision, and validation targets differ in concrete ways. The phase-field surrogate of Montes de Oca Zapiain \emph{et al.} evolves a global low-dimensional representation of simulated two-phase spinodal decomposition. The original PRIMME learns two-dimensional Monte Carlo Potts neighbor transitions, whereas GrainGNN uses phase-field-labeled, hand-crafted grain/junction graphs to predict three-dimensional epitaxial grain formation. The autoencoder--ConvLSTM model of Tep and Bernacki operates on simulator-generated two-dimensional image sequences and emphasizes image similarity, mean size, distributions, and computational acceleration. A fixed three-dimensional local operator, by comparison, decouples the learned input size from the global inference volume, turns one volume into many boundary-centered examples, and can be evaluated through categorical voxel transitions as well as kinetics, topology, morphology, and inclination response. The completed 3D-PRIMME work tests this latter hypothesis explicitly through rollout, spatial scaling, topology, and prescribed-anisotropy experiments.

This dissertation does not claim that local learning replaces phase-field, graph, or other surrogate formulations. Its contribution is a source-aware program organized around three questions: which local rule is present in the labels, whether that rule can be learned and scaled, and what additional evidence becomes necessary when supervision changes from a controlled simulator to sparse 4D measurement. In the experimental setting, window selection, label-preserving curation, simple null models, replicate and readout sensitivity, and field-level artifact diagnostics are part of the scientific method, not auxiliary preprocessing.

\section{Research question and central hypothesis}

This dissertation asks how local, physics-guided machine learning can progress from learning physically controlled simulation dynamics to learning reliably from sparse 4D experimental observations, and ultimately support physical interpretation of mesoscale grain growth. The hypothesis is that a local operator can learn grain evolution directly from a curated 4D experiment, and that what it learns can then be tested for physical content rather than presumed to carry it.

The hypothesis does not assume that agreement with a simulator proves agreement with nature, that one selected experimental window establishes generalization, or that accurate prediction automatically identifies a mechanism. Its experimental claim is bounded to the selected field of view and the observed trajectory.

It has operational failure conditions. It will not be supported if selection provenance or field-level artifact diagnostics are inadequate; if T2--T4 results do not improve over the relevant simple baselines; or if interpretation signatures are unstable across model initializations, experimental states, geometric strata, and controlled tests. The first failure mode is not hypothetical: an uncurated model produced roughly \(3.5\) voxels per step of rigid drift while its population statistics still looked favorable~\cite{tian2026experiment}.

The question is addressed through a sequence of progressively removed assumptions:

\begin{enumerate}

\item diagnose how a stochastic simulation neighborhood determines the physical content and artifacts of simulation labels;

\item test whether a scalable local surrogate can learn those controlled simulation rules;

\item replace simulation labels with curated experimental evolution;

\item evaluate the resulting operator against later experimental states that were held out from fitting, while stating the limits of their prior use in the ongoing analysis;

\item interpret only those experimentally learned relationships that pass the relevant reliability tests.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Dissertation logic and evidence ladder}

The first two stages of that sequence are completed foundations. They establish that local simulation rules can be physically diagnosed and that a compact local architecture can learn and scale those rules. Aim 1 then combines selection, curation, linkage, and direct T0--T1 training in one experimental field of view with retrospective evaluation on later states that were held out from fitting but not from subsequent analysis. Changing the label generator removes the simulator from the supervision path, but it does not identify physical content absent from the simulator. Aim 2 therefore interprets only the experimental outputs supported by both field-integrity and temporal evidence, as summarized in Figure~\ref{fig:proposal-framework}.

\chapter{Completed Foundations: From Simulation Physics to 3D Surrogate Learning}

\section{Neighborhood-dependent simulation physics}
\label{sec:neighborhood-physics}

\subsection{Stochastic update rules and neighborhood geometry}

Stochastic lattice and voxel models represent a microstructure through discrete grain identities and update those identities according to local rules. Their computational accessibility makes them useful for generating long sequences and large training sets, but their effective behavior depends on more than the nominal physical objective. Neighborhood shape, lattice discretization, sampling count, pseudo-temperature, update acceptance, and spatially varying mobility each leave a mark on the resulting inclination preference, pinning, morphology, topology, and grain-level statistical scatter~\cite{wu1982potts,anderson1984kinetics,srolovitz1984topology,holm1998mobility,melville2024modefilter,tian2026neighborhood}.

The conventional Monte Carlo Potts model evaluates a local Hamiltonian in a fixed neighborhood, proposes an identity change, and accepts or rejects it based on the resulting energy change and pseudo-temperature. For site \(i\), the local energy and acceptance rule are

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{H}_i
&=
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_i}
\gamma_{ij}\left(1-\delta_{s_i,s_j}\right),\\
\Delta\mathcal{H}_i
&=
\mathcal{H}'_i-\mathcal{H}_i,\\
p(\Delta\mathcal{H}_i)
&=
\begin{cases}
\exp\!\left(-\dfrac{\Delta\mathcal{H}_i}{kT}\right),
& \Delta\mathcal{H}_i>0,\\[4pt]
1, & \Delta\mathcal{H}_i\le 0,
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:mcp-update}
\end{equation}

Here, \(s_i\) is the grain identity at site \(i\), \(\mathcal{N}_i\) is its neighborhood, \(\gamma_{ij}\) is the relative boundary energy, \(T\) is the pseudo-temperature, and \(\delta\) is the Kronecker delta. The mode filter instead samples sites from a positive symmetric kernel and assigns the most frequent sampled identity. Neighborhood-driven MCP replaces the fixed Moore neighborhood with samples from an arbitrary probability mass function while retaining the MCP acceptance rule. These methods provide a controlled setting in which the influence of neighborhood geometry can be separated from other parts of the update policy~\cite{wu1982potts,anderson1984kinetics,melville2024modefilter,tian2026neighborhood}.

Lattice pinning has been recognized for decades, and several mitigations are in routine use: raising the pseudo-temperature so that fluctuations roughen boundaries off the lattice, switching to a triangular or hexagonal lattice whose symmetry matches the equilibrium \(120^\circ\) triple-junction angle, enlarging the neighborhood used to evaluate the Hamiltonian, or weighting transitions with a truncated radial basis function~\cite{holm1998mobility,zollner2014temperature,mason2015kinetics}. Each reduces the artifact. None explains what produces it, and the more effective ones raise the cost of the simulation substantially. That gap matters here for a specific reason: a mitigation that is not understood cannot be inverted, and this dissertation needs to prescribe a directional response deliberately, not only suppress an unwanted one.

The neighborhood study investigates why stochastic grain-growth models develop lattice pinning and inclination-dependent artifacts. Its central contribution is to show that the local neighborhood is not a neutral numerical stencil. The first absolute moment of the sampling kernel defines an effective inclination-dependent interfacial energy, which can be connected through a Wulff construction to equilibrium shape and inclination distribution. Discrete sampling and stochastic update policy determine how sharply that theoretical response is expressed~\cite{tian2026neighborhood}.

For a continuous two-dimensional kernel \(K(x,y)\), the inclination-dependent interfacial energy and its discrete-lattice counterpart are

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\gamma(\theta)
&=
\int_{\mathbb{R}^2}
K(x,y)\,
\left|x\cos\theta+y\sin\theta\right|
\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y,\\
\gamma_d(\theta)
&=
\sum_{m=1}^{M}
K(x_m,y_m)\,
\left|x_m\cos\theta+y_m\sin\theta\right|.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:kernel-energy}
\end{equation}

Thus, neighborhood geometry enters the effective physics through a directional first absolute moment. The corresponding equilibrium Wulff shape is

\begin{equation}
W=
\bigcap_{\theta\in(0,2\pi]}
\left\{
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2:
x\cos\theta+y\sin\theta\le\gamma(\theta)
\right\}.
\label{eq:wulff}
\end{equation}

Equations~\eqref{eq:kernel-energy}--\eqref{eq:wulff} make the causal chain explicit: changing \(K\) changes \(\gamma(\theta)\), which changes the preferred equilibrium shape and its boundary-inclination population~\cite{eggleston2001anisotropic,taylor1978crystalline}.

The Wulff shape is not itself the quantity measured in a simulated microstructure. To close the chain onto an observable, the completed study converts the shape into the inclination population it would generate. For a convex \(\gamma(\theta)\), the boundary normal \(\phi(\theta)\) and the arc length \(\ell(\theta)\) carried at that normal are

\begin{equation}
\phi(\theta)
=
\arctan\!
\left(
\frac{\mathrm{d}\left[\gamma(\theta)\sin\theta\right]/\mathrm{d}\theta}
{\mathrm{d}\left[\gamma(\theta)\cos\theta\right]/\mathrm{d}\theta}
\right),
\qquad
\ell(\theta)
=
\sqrt{
\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\left[\gamma(\theta)\sin\theta\right]}{\mathrm{d}\theta}\right)^{2}
+
\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\left[\gamma(\theta)\cos\theta\right]}{\mathrm{d}\theta}\right)^{2}
}\,.
\label{eq:inclination-from-wulff}
\end{equation}

Binning \(\phi(\theta)\) and accumulating \(\ell(\theta)\) within each bin gives the predicted inclination distribution, which is directly comparable with the distribution measured from a simulated microstructure by the boundary-smoothing algorithm of Yang et al.~\cite{yang2022inclination}. A kernel thus yields a falsifiable prediction, not only a qualitative expectation.

Four neighborhoods were chosen to span the range from isotropic to strongly directional, and each admits a closed-form \(\gamma(\theta)\) (Figure~\ref{fig:neighborhood-kernel-wulff} and Table~\ref{tab:kernel-energies}). The progression is deliberate: the Gaussian kernel gives an angle-independent interfacial energy and a circular Wulff shape; squaring the Gaussian through the coordinate transformation of Fong~\cite{fong2015squaring} flattens the energy near the diagonals; the compact square kernel produces a rounded diamond whose highest-energy directions lie along the lattice axes; and the star kernel, whose mass sits on four line segments, produces an eightfold response and a rounded-octagon Wulff shape.

Discretization sets a floor on how finely this prediction can be expressed. On a lattice the integral in Equation~\eqref{eq:kernel-energy} becomes the sum \(\gamma_d(\theta)=\sum_m K(x_m,y_m)\left|x_m\cos\theta+y_m\sin\theta\right|\), and for a small kernel the spatial discretization quantizes the accessible angles. The Wulff construction for a \(3\times3\) uniform kernel is an octagon, so the energy-minimizing solution admits only eight inclinations, one per neighborhood direction~\cite{qiu2024cylindrical}. As the kernel support grows beyond the lattice spacing, \(\gamma_d(\theta)\) becomes a Riemann approximation of the continuous \(\gamma(\theta)\), and the theoretical response is recovered. This argument is independent of pseudo-temperature: \(T\) changes how an algorithm approaches the energy-minimizing shape, not which shape it approaches.

The update policy governs that approach. At \(T=0\) in MCP, a central site belonging to the minority grain has only one energetically valid move, so a two-grain interface advances deterministically toward the majority grain; at a triple junction the randomly drawn neighbor may belong to either majority grain, and the resulting mixing slows convergence to the Wulff shape without preventing it. Raising \(T\) admits unfavorable flips, which blurs the effective kernel, but the neighborhood still sets the anisotropy because samples along the diagonals lie farther from the center and so imply a faster interface velocity. The mode filter behaves differently again: as the number of sampled sites \(M\) grows, the sampling estimate converges to the convolution that defines the Hamiltonian, so MF approaches the theoretical response more directly than either MCP variant.

The same directional-moment argument extends to a three-dimensional kernel. For unit interface normal \(\hat{\mathbf n}\),

\begin{equation}
\gamma(\hat{\mathbf n})
=
\int_{\mathbb{R}^3}
K(\mathbf r)
\left|\mathbf r\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\right|
\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf r,
\qquad
W_3
=
\bigcap_{\hat{\mathbf n}\in\mathbb{S}^2}
\left\{
\mathbf x\in\mathbb{R}^3:
\mathbf x\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\le\gamma(\hat{\mathbf n})
\right\}.
\label{eq:kernel-energy-3d}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:kernel-energy-3d} supplies the dimensional bridge used in the dissertation: a three-dimensional sampling distribution prescribes a directional response over interface normals rather than planar angles. A literal three-dimensional extension of a planar star kernel would inherit the symmetry of its selected arms, commonly producing axial or cubic preferences instead of rotational invariance. The downstream 3D-PRIMME study instead uses Gaussian kernels whose covariance provides a compact, controlled way to impose isotropic or \([111]\)-biased sampling.

For surrogate learning, this result reframes simulator design as training-data governance. A neural operator trained on simulation does not see an abstract law; it sees updates generated by a particular kernel and update policy. Artificial directional preference in the simulator becomes an artificial directional preference available for learning. Conversely, a deliberately anisotropic neighborhood can provide controlled labels for testing whether a model recovers a prescribed response.

\subsection{Methods}

The completed study compares MCP, N-MCP, and MF evolution in \(2400\times2400\)-pixel domains initialized from a common 20,000-grain Voronoi tessellation. Comparisons are performed at matched grain counts because neighborhood choice changes the evolution rate. Gaussian, reshaped-Gaussian, square, and star-shaped sampling neighborhoods are used to vary inclination dependence systematically. The analysis evaluates theoretical and simulated inclination distributions, aggregate anisotropy magnitude, equilibrium morphology, rotation of the imposed anisotropy, von Neumann--Mullins behavior, statistical scatter, and computational cost~\cite{tian2026neighborhood}.

N-MCP is the minimal modification that isolates the neighborhood from every other part of the MCP update. Only the first step changes: instead of evaluating the Hamiltonian over a fixed Moore neighborhood, the algorithm draws \(M\) sites from the kernel \(K\), computes \(\mathcal{H}_i\) over that random neighborhood, proposes one of its labels, and accepts or rejects by the Metropolis rule of Equation~\eqref{eq:mcp-update}. The site itself is excluded from sampling. Because acceptance, pseudo-temperature, and lattice are all untouched, any change in the measured inclination response is attributable to the neighborhood alone.

The comparison is run under matched conditions. MF with the Gaussian kernel evolves for 2,000 unitless time steps, and the reshaped-Gaussian and uniform kernels for 4,000, since the non-circular neighborhoods coarsen more slowly; MCP and N-MCP run for 1,600 steps. Gaussian sampling uses a variance of 25 in both dimensions, and the uniform kernel spans \([-10,10]\) so that the two neighborhoods cover comparable area at the \(95\%\) level of the Gaussian. MCP is run in SPPARKS~\cite{plimpton2009spparks} from the same initial structure at \(kT=0.66\), the value reported to avoid lattice pinning~\cite{zollner2014temperature}; N-MCP samples from a Gaussian with \(\sigma=0.5\), so that \(2\sigma\) and \(3\sigma\) correspond to the \(3\times3\) and \(5\times5\) neighborhoods used in MCP. Rotation tests apply a rotation matrix to the sampled offsets at \(0^\circ\), \(30^\circ\), and \(60^\circ\), leaving the pixel grid fixed.

Anisotropy is reduced to one scalar so that neighborhoods can be ranked and tracked through coarsening:

\begin{equation}
A
=
\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{bins}}}
\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{bins}}}
\frac{\left|f_i-f_{\mathrm{iso}}\right|}{f_{\mathrm{iso}}},
\label{eq:anisotropy-magnitude}
\end{equation}
where \(f_i\) is the measured frequency in inclination bin \(i\) and \(f_{\mathrm{iso}}\) is the frequency an isotropic distribution would place in every bin. Values near zero indicate an inclination population with no preferred direction.

\subsection{Completed results}

Conventional zero-temperature MCP exhibits strong lattice-aligned boundary populations, visible both as boundaries clustered near \(\pm45^\circ\) from the major axes and as peaks at those angles in the inclination distribution. Raising the pseudo-temperature to \(kT=0.66\) softens but does not remove the response. N-MCP with Gaussian sampling shows no visually detectable pinning and an effectively circular inclination distribution. Progressing from Gaussian to reshaped Gaussian, square, and star neighborhoods produces progressively stronger directional preferences, with the star case adding preferences at \(0^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\) and grains that visibly approach octagons. MF exhibits the same ordering, although its anisotropy is generally weaker than N-MCP's at matched neighborhood~\cite{tian2026neighborhood}.

Equation~\eqref{eq:anisotropy-magnitude} puts numbers on that ordering and shows that it persists through coarsening. MCP holds an anisotropy magnitude near \(0.15\), roughly constant until the population falls below 6,000 grains. MF with Gaussian sampling holds near \(0.01\) over the same range, more than an order of magnitude lower, and rises only once the population drops below 6,000 grains and the domain no longer contains enough boundaries for a stable estimate. The reshaped Gaussian rises to about \(0.05\) by 10,000 grains and resumes rising below 6,000; the uniform kernel starts near \(0.05\) and exceeds MCP itself by 6,000 grains. N-MCP holds its low anisotropy across the tested variances and temperatures, which is the practical statement of the claim: the neighborhood, and not the pseudo-temperature, is the control variable~\cite{tian2026neighborhood}.

The rotation test provides the clearest causal evidence (Figure~\ref{fig:neighborhood-rotation}). Rotating reshaped-Gaussian and uniform MF neighborhoods by 0, 30, and 60 degrees rotates the measured inclination response by the same angle at a fixed population of 8,369 grains, while the anisotropy magnitude changes only slightly. Since the pixel grid is identical in every run, the orientation of the response follows the neighborhood and not the lattice, which is the result the term ``lattice pinning'' would not predict.

Neighborhood geometry and algorithmic stochasticity turn out to govern different observables, and separating them is what makes the mode filter the right label source for the surrogate work. Against the von Neumann--Mullins relation, evaluated at approximately 12,590 grains, MCP reproduces the expected mean but with large grain-to-grain scatter. N-MCP is no better, and is in fact worse: excluding the center site leaves each site with between zero and eight actual samples, so the effective neighborhood size itself fluctuates. Raising the sample count to 16 and then 64, and fixing it exactly, changes the result only marginally. MF instead shows much smaller scatter at every neighborhood tested, and the residual departure of its uniform-kernel mean appears only for grains with many faces. The difference traces to the update rule: MF selects the majority label over the whole sampled neighborhood, whereas both MCP variants accept or reject a single randomly drawn label. Since the sampled mean over \(N\) independent draws has variance \(\sigma^{2}/N\), adherence improves as sampling grows, which the study confirms across 4, 9, 16, 25, 64, and 128 samples~\cite{tian2026neighborhood}.

Cost decides which of these is usable as a label generator at scale. Evolving the same initial structure from 20,000 to 2,000 grains takes 34 seconds in MCP, 1 minute 16 seconds in MF, and 23 minutes 58 seconds in N-MCP. MCP is the cheapest but is not lattice free; N-MCP removes the lattice artifact at roughly twenty times the cost of MF and retains high von Neumann--Mullins variance. MF is the only one of the three that is simultaneously lattice free, statistically well behaved, and cheap enough to generate the hundreds of three-dimensional sequences the surrogate work requires. That is the reason 3D-PRIMME is trained on mode-filter data.

This work establishes control with respect to lattice pinning, artificial inclination preference, and selected topological/statistical behavior. It does not establish correct material-specific mobility, misorientation dependence, boundary energy, or agreement with experimental alumina. The defensible dissertation claim is therefore that the neighborhood study provides the physical basis for selecting and diagnosing the simulated label source used for surrogate training. It should not be claimed that the study directly proved improved 3D-PRIMME accuracy, because no surrogate was trained on paired ``poor'' and ``improved'' neighborhood datasets. Its enabling role is conceptual and methodological: it identifies which local rules are present in the simulation labels and provides controlled isotropic and anisotropic label sources for the next stage.

\section{Simulation-trained 3D-PRIMME}

A local surrogate represents evolution through a fixed neighborhood operator instead of a global map over the entire volume. This creates three potential advantages. First, a small three-dimensional volume contains many local boundary-centered training examples. Second, the learned operator can be applied to a larger domain because its input size does not depend on global volume dimensions. Third, repeated application provides temporal rollouts beyond the training interval. These benefits are meaningful only if locality retains sufficient boundary context and if rollout errors do not accumulate into incorrect kinetics or topology~\cite{montes2021surrogate,yan2022primme,qin2024graingnn,tep2025grain,tian2026threeDprimme}.

Validation must therefore span voxel and boundary-update agreement, field morphology and rigid drift, population kinetics, normalized grain-size distributions, topology and face-count behavior, individual-grain growth or shrinkage, and inclination-dependent response where supported. These observables are organized by evidentiary strength in Table~\ref{tab:evidence-ladder}.
Agreement at one level does not imply agreement at another. Direct simulation--experiment studies and multi-model comparisons show that one description may reproduce size statistics while missing shape, topology, interface curvature, or individual-grain evolution~\cite{mckenna2014fourD,rowenhorst2010topology,martine2019metrics}. A learned rollout can likewise reproduce a size distribution while making incorrect individual updates or retaining the wrong topology~\cite{tian2026threeDprimme,tian2026experiment}.

\subsection{Architecture and simulation supervision}

3D-PRIMME learns three-dimensional grain evolution from local boundary geometry. Grain identities are transformed into an interface-site representation that counts unlike neighbors in a local observation window. At site \(i\) and time \(t\), the representation and the candidate-update target are

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
S_i^{(t)}
&=
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_o(i)}
\left(1-\delta_{ij}^{(t)}\right),
\qquad
\delta_{ij}^{(t)}
=
\mathbf{1}\!\left[s_i^{(t)}=s_j^{(t)}\right],\\
y_{ij}^{(t)}
&=
\begin{cases}
1, & s_i^{(t+1)}=s_j^{(t)},\\
0, & s_i^{(t+1)}\ne s_j^{(t)},
\end{cases}
\qquad j\in\mathcal{N}_a(i).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:primme-representation}
\end{equation}

Here, \(\mathcal{N}_o(i)\) and \(\mathcal{N}_a(i)\) are the observation and action windows. The network \(Y_\vartheta\) is fitted with the local squared-error objective

\begin{equation}
L_i^{(t)}
=
\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{N}_a(i)\right|}
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_a(i)}
\left|
Y_\vartheta\!\left(S_i^{(t)},S_j^{(t)}\right)
-y_{ij}^{(t)}
\right|^2 .
\label{eq:primme-loss}
\end{equation}

Boundary-centered patches are extracted, and the trained network maps each local representation to candidate grain-identity update scores within the action window. Local predictions are assembled into the next global grain-ID map, and the operator is then applied autoregressively (Figure~\ref{fig:primme-workflow}). Because the observation and action windows have fixed size, the learned operator is not tied to the global training-volume dimensions~\cite{tian2026threeDprimme}.

The two windows are separate design parameters because they act at different stages. The observation window \(N_o\) is applied before the network, when the grain-ID field is converted into the interface-site representation, so it can be chosen from a physical length scale such as the mean grain size. The action window \(N_a\) is applied after that conversion and fixes the input dimension of the network. Decoupling them allows the physical context used to build the representation to be set independently of the size of the learned mapping, which is what lets one architecture serve microstructures at different length scales.

One architectural change from the two-dimensional formulation is worth stating explicitly, because it bears on the interpretability work in Aim 2. The original 2D PRIMME required a regularization term that penalized increases in the number of grain-boundary sites in order to keep long rollouts stable~\cite{yan2022primme}. 3D-PRIMME removes that term and remains stable. The interface-site representation, together with the finite observation and action windows, already restricts what the network can express to the local grain-boundary environment, so the constraint that had to be imposed through the loss in two dimensions is supplied by the representation itself in three~\cite{tian2026threeDprimme}.

Training and evaluation use three-dimensional MF data, chosen as the label source for three reasons. The stochasticity of the MF update introduces fluctuations that stand in, imperfectly, for the noise of an experimental sequence. The kinetics and the directional response are both controllable through the sampling kernel, which is what makes prescribed-anisotropy tests possible at all. And the algorithm is cheap enough to generate the required volume of three-dimensional data, as the runtime comparison in Section~\ref{sec:neighborhood-physics} showed. The reported dataset contains 200 isotropic sequences and a corresponding set of inclination-dependent sequences. Each sequence starts from a different 512-grain Voronoi structure in a \(100^3\)-voxel volume and evolves for 100 simulation steps, while a training set uses only two consecutive states from one or more sequences. For the inclination-dependent data, the Gaussian sampling covariance is

\[
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
a & b & b\\
b & a & b\\
b & b & a
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
a=25,\quad b=20,
\]
which biases the MF neighborhood along
\(\mathbf{u}_{111}=(1,1,1)/\sqrt{3}\). Between them, the isotropic and inclination-dependent datasets provide controlled label sources for testing whether the learned local operator recovers both scalar coarsening behavior and a prescribed directional response. No experimental microstructure is used in this completed study~\cite{tian2026threeDprimme}.
This covariance construction applies the neighborhood-design principle of Section~\ref{sec:neighborhood-physics} directly in three dimensions: the sampling distribution defines the directional response available to the surrogate. The completed studies do not contain an ablation that pairs a poor label source against an improved one, but they do demonstrate the intended sequence from a diagnosed three-dimensional label source to a learned anisotropic response.

\subsection{Completed performance}

Window-sensitivity tests show how local context affects the learned kinetics. All evaluated settings recover approximately linear coarsening, but the observation window affects the growth rate more strongly than the action window, and it does so in a physically legible direction. Observation windows smaller than the relevant boundary environment underpredict the MF kinetics; windows larger than it accelerate coarsening. The action window produces a weaker and less systematic effect on growth rate, which follows from its role: once \(N_o\) has encoded the physical context, enlarging the network input adds no new information. Across the sweep, every \(N_o=9\) case falls between 2.85\% and 3.49\% relative error in \(\left\langle r\right\rangle^2\) against the MF reference, and both \(N_o=7\) and \(N_o=11\) are generally worse. The \(N_o=9\), \(N_a=9\) setting produces the smallest kinetic error, 2.85\%, and is used as the default throughout~\cite{tian2026threeDprimme}.

Voxel accuracy falls with rollout time in every configuration. That decay is not by itself evidence of incorrect physics. The reference is a stochastic MF trajectory while the surrogate is deterministic, so the two diverge from the shared initial condition even when both remain physically valid, and ensemble studies of polycrystalline grain growth report the same limit on trajectory-level predictability once abrupt topological events such as grain disappearance begin to occur~\cite{lyu2026predictivity}. Voxel accuracy is read here as fidelity to the training reference, with the kinetic, topological, and distributional metrics carrying the physical claim.

Ten models trained on the same single two-state sequence, differing only in initialization and data shuffling, show small spread in squared mean radius, average face count, the topology-size relation, and voxel accuracy, although the spread grows with rollout time. Varying initialization alone and shuffling alone were also tested separately, and the mean trends agree across all three settings, so the reported variability is not an artifact of one randomness source. The residual spread is read as epistemic uncertainty from finite data and parameter non-uniqueness~\cite{hullermeier2021uncertainty}, and it manifests as long-horizon trajectory divergence of the kind expected from autoregressive surrogates~\cite{mccabe2023stability,tian2026threeDprimme}.

Two of the quantitative readouts can be written as

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\left\langle r(t)\right\rangle^2
&\approx
\left\langle r(0)\right\rangle^2+K_g t,\\
\operatorname{Acc}(t)
&=
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\mathbf{1}\!\left[\hat{s}_i^{(t)}=s_i^{(t)}\right].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:kinetics-accuracy}
\end{equation}
where the first relation is the parabolic coarsening diagnostic, \(K_g\) is the fitted growth-rate coefficient, and the second is voxel-wise agreement between predicted and reference grain labels. The coarsening relation is used as a kinetic diagnostic, not as a claim of universal continuous-time calibration.

The distinction between training support and inference scale is central to the spatial and temporal extrapolation demonstrated by 3D-PRIMME. The network is trained from local patches extracted from \(100^3\)-voxel volumes and requires only one transition between two consecutive states. During inference, the same fixed local operator is applied repeatedly and independently of the global domain dimensions. A model trained at \(100^3\) is applied without retraining to \(256^3\), \(512^3\), and \(1024^3\) domains containing approximately 8,600, 68,700, and 550,000 initial grains. The grain counts are chosen so that the mean initial grain size is the same in every domain, which makes the comparison a test of domain size alone and not of a changed microstructural state~\cite{tian2026threeDprimme}.

After a short transient associated with the Voronoi initial condition, \(\left\langle r\right\rangle^2\) grows approximately linearly and nearly identically in all three domains. The average face count rises quickly to a common steady value and collapses onto a single curve when plotted against \(\left\langle r\right\rangle^2\); the steady value is consistent with the 13 to 14 faces per grain expected for ideal three-dimensional isotropic growth~\cite{wakai2000threeD,kamachali2012topology}. Normalized grain-radius distributions also agree across domain sizes at the two matched coarsening states, \(N_G\approx N_{G,0}/2\) and \(N_G\approx N_{G,0}/4\). The demonstrated ``temporal extrapolation'' is autoregressive deployment beyond the two-state training interval within the MF setting; it is not a claim of calibrated physical-time prediction for an unseen material.

Data efficiency under limited temporal supervision is evaluated by training on 1, 10, or 50 two-state sequences and comparing against an independent set of ten MF simulations under a fixed number of optimizer updates. All three training-set sizes preserve approximately linear coarsening and similar face-count behavior. Ten sequences give the highest voxel accuracy and smallest replicate spread in that comparison, whereas the 50-sequence case coarsens more slowly and shows larger variability. The latter observation is treated as a training-design result, possibly reflecting redundancy, stochastic diversity, or insufficient optimization under the fixed update budget. It is not evidence that additional data are intrinsically harmful~\cite{tian2026threeDprimme}.

The deeper reason the model tolerates so little temporal supervision is that it learns a local update rule and not a trajectory. A single \(100^3\) volume already contains a very large number of boundary-centered neighborhoods, so the training set is highly redundant: once the dominant local transition patterns are sampled, further sequences add optimization examples without adding much independent information. Dimensionality compounds this. The two-dimensional formulation required 200 sequences of five consecutive steps, whereas the three-dimensional model reaches stable predictions from one or ten sequences of two steps, because a 3D microstructure of comparable linear size contains far more local neighborhoods, interface geometries, and topological environments than a 2D one~\cite{yan2022primme,tian2026threeDprimme}.

A separate inclination-dependent test evaluates whether the surrogate recovers more than scalar coarsening statistics. The anisotropic MF kernel produces an evolving directional signature, but boundary inclination is not supplied to 3D-PRIMME as an explicit input feature. Nevertheless, the initially near-circular distributions develop the expected elongated response, and the 3D-PRIMME curves remain close to the MF reference in the XY, XZ, and YZ projection planes through rollout step 50 (Figure~\ref{fig:primme-inclination}). The distributions are measured with the same boundary-smoothing algorithm used in the neighborhood study~\cite{yang2022inclination,yang2024triplejunction}, so the simulation and surrogate are held to one definition of inclination. This agreement indicates that the interface-site representation retains sufficient local geometry for the network to infer the mode filter's direction-dependent update rule. It also sets up a concrete extension: an explicit misorientation representation has already been demonstrated in two dimensions~\cite{melville2024anisotropic}, and adding one here would give a fully anisotropic three-dimensional operator accounting for both inclination and misorientation~\cite{tian2026threeDprimme}.

The completed study demonstrates that a compact local representation contains sufficient information to reproduce major kinetic, topological, and inclination-dependent features of the MF reference over spatial and temporal scales larger than those used for training. It also establishes that local context must be selected carefully: locality does not mean that an arbitrarily small or large neighborhood is equally informative.

The claim is nevertheless bounded by the supervision source. The model learns MF evolution, not universal grain-growth truth. Its geometry-only representation does not explicitly encode crystallographic misorientation, boundary energy, or mobility. Its successful recovery of anisotropy demonstrates learning of a prescribed simulation response, not discovery of an unknown experimental mechanism~\cite{tian2026threeDprimme}.

\section{The reach and the ceiling of the completed foundations}

A simulation-trained surrogate is constrained twice: first by the simulator's representation of the material and then by the approximation error of the learned model. Improving agreement between 3D-PRIMME and MF reduces the second error but cannot remove the first. A high-fidelity surrogate of an idealized simulator remains an idealized model.
The completed studies make this distinction observable. The neighborhood work shows that local kernels can introduce, suppress, or prescribe inclination dependence. 3D-PRIMME then shows that a neural operator can recover the resulting simulated response. Together, they demonstrate both the strength and the ceiling of simulation supervision: controlled rules can be learned and scaled, but missing or incorrectly represented mechanisms cannot be recovered from labels that do not contain them.

Direct experimental learning matters because it tests whether measured evolution can supervise the same local architecture, not because it supplies additional patches. It changes the label source from a prescribed stochastic model to measured microstructure evolution, and so removes the simulator from the label-generating path. This shift does \emph{not} by itself demonstrate that the experiment-trained operator has recovered material-specific physics absent from MF; establishing that difference would require a calibrated simulation--experiment comparison that is outside the core scope of this dissertation. Experimental labels also introduce uncertainty, incomplete observability, and acquisition artifacts. The next challenge is to determine whether those labels can support a reliable local operator without confusing measurement structure with physical evolution.

Relative to a controlled simulation, the completed studies establish label and field integrity together with mesoscale predictive fidelity. They do not establish experimental fidelity, stable model interpretation, physical attribution, or a candidate mechanism. The experimental work retains the same dependency: each stronger claim is conditional on the evidence required by the preceding stage.

\section{Challenges of learning from sparse 4D experiments}

Experimental grain-ID maps are not automatically valid training pairs. Rigid translation and rotation produce apparent boundary displacement. Residual non-rigid distortion can create a spatially varying update field. Surface recession changes the common material volume. Grain identities can be lost or incorrectly linked between scans. Small grains may fall below detection thresholds, and segmentation churn can create or remove apparent grains. If these effects are passed directly to an autoregressive model, the network can learn them as spurious dynamics~\cite{tian2026experiment}.

Label-preserving preprocessing is part of the scientific method here. Continuous interpolation is inappropriate for categorical grain identities because it produces values that do not correspond to grains. Registration must move whole labels, preserve identity, and expose residual uncertainty instead of hiding it through smoothing.
Unlike independent label noise, these effects can produce a spatially organized apparent update rule that is repeatedly amplified during autoregressive rollout. Direction-sensitive registration residuals and rollout drift are therefore as important as aggregate fit metrics.

Non-destructive X-ray grain mapping has progressed from synchrotron structural microscopy and direct observations of bulk-grain evolution to synchrotron and laboratory diffraction-contrast tomography~\cite{larson2002xray,schmidt2004watching,ludwig2008dct,king2013labdct,mcdonald2015labdct}. These methods now supply voxelized three-dimensional grain maps at successive states and have enabled time-resolved studies of grain growth and related microstructural evolution~\cite{mckenna2014fourD,mcdonald2017sintering,zhang2018grain,wang2025abnormal}. The present dataset is spatially rich but temporally sparse: one pair of volumes contains hundreds of thousands of local boundary-centered patches, but the dataset contains only five independent time states. This asymmetry makes local learning feasible while limiting claims of generalization. Overlapping patches from one transition increase the optimization sample count, but they do not replace independent experimental windows or time intervals. Random voxel-level train/validation splits test whether the architecture fits the selected pair; they do not demonstrate cross-window or cross-material transfer, consistent with the known optimism of random validation when samples retain spatial or temporal dependence~\cite{roberts2017crossvalidation,tian2026experiment}.
The appropriate unit of inference is an experimental partition or coherent unit---a window, time pair, spatial block, or linked grain---rather than an individual overlapping voxel. Within the available single trajectory, Aim 1 therefore treats the random patch split only as optimization monitoring and summarizes robustness across model initializations and coherent spatial or grain-level units.

Experimental reliability cannot be decided with one scalar score. The current study shows why. Grain-size distributions can agree while topology remains biased; an apparently stable rollout can miss late experimental slowdown; aggregate coarsening can be plausible while individual-grain magnitude is underpredicted. Most directly, a model trained on the same experimental window without label curation retained plausible population-level statistics while its recursive rollout developed a coherent rigid translation of approximately \(3.5\) voxels per step (Fig.~\ref{fig:experimental-curation}). Directional drift reveals a learned registration artifact that aggregate statistics miss. The proposed work will preserve a multilevel evaluation matrix instead of collapsing all outcomes into a single accuracy number~\cite{tian2026experiment}.

This multilevel strategy is consistent with the distinction between aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty in machine learning, practical ensemble-based uncertainty estimation, and evidence that both dataset shift and autoregressive rollout require assessment beyond one-step fit~\cite{hullermeier2021uncertainty,lakshminarayanan2017ensembles,ovadia2019shift,mccabe2023stability}. It also reflects simulation--experiment comparisons and experimental grain-growth studies showing that size statistics, topology, curvature, boundary character, and measured grain-volume change need not reduce to one scalar relation~\cite{mckenna2014fourD,rowenhorst2010topology,martine2019metrics,bhattacharya2021curvature,peng2022comparison,rohrer2011anisotropy,han2018kinetics}.

\section{Aim 1: Establish and evaluate direct experimental learning from a curated 4D trajectory}

\subsection{Question and hypothesis}

Can a residual-driven, label-preserving workflow select and curate one experimental 4D window well enough for a local surrogate to learn grain evolution without learning acquisition motion as dynamics, and to what extent does the resulting operator reproduce the measured T2--T4 evolution in the same field of view?

If the selected field of view is registered by whole-label transformations, linked before local target construction, and audited with direction-sensitive residual and rollout-drift diagnostics, then the unmodified local architecture can learn a stable experimental transition from T0 to T1. On T2--T4, the resulting operator will reproduce field morphology, early coarsening kinetics, and normalized grain-size statistics more faithfully than topology and individual-grain volume change. Comparisons with persistence, unchanged-distribution, majority-class, and readout-sensitivity baselines will distinguish incremental predictive information from agreement that follows from slow or self-similar evolution. The resulting claim is deliberately local: Aim 1 establishes data integrity, direct-training feasibility, and a bounded fidelity profile in the selected trajectory, not spatial reproducibility across experimental windows.

\subsection{Rationale and preliminary evidence}

Direct experimental supervision changes the label source from a controlled simulator to measured evolution, but it also makes label construction part of the scientific model. A rigid or spatially varying registration residual can become a coherent learned update and then amplify under autoregressive rollout. Aim 1 therefore treats window selection, categorical registration, grain linkage, and drift auditing as components of the learning method rather than as incidental preprocessing. The work uses one field of view because that is the experimental trajectory selected and analyzed in the ongoing study; no cross-window claim is made.

T2--T4 were excluded from model fitting, so they test temporal extrapolation beyond the T0--T1 training pair. They are not a new confirmatory partition: all five states contributed to the common interior-volume definition, and the later states have already been examined in the ongoing study. The temporal component of Aim 1 is therefore framed as retrospective evaluation on states held out from fitting within one selected field of view. This distinction prevents temporal holdout from being overstated as independent system validation.

The existing results already span favorable and unfavorable outcomes. Rollouts remain morphologically coherent and reproduce early aggregate coarsening and normalized size-distribution shape, while late-time kinetics, topology, and individual-grain magnitude show visible limitations~\cite{tian2026experiment}. Aim 1 consolidates those results under the common matrix of Table~\ref{tab:aim1-reliability-matrix} and completes the persistence, majority-class, unchanged-distribution, and readout-sensitivity comparisons needed to state what information the model adds beyond simple baselines.

\subsection{Curation, direct training, and field-integrity results}

The ongoing study uses five laboratory-DCT grain-ID reconstructions acquired after cumulative holds of 8, 10, 12, 14, and 16 hours. The full reconstruction contains \(549\times149\times211\) voxels. Window selection uses only the tracked T0--T1 pair and proceeds in two stages. A \(100^3\) window is first scanned over the full admissible corner domain at a 25-voxel stride: \(x=0,25,\ldots,425\), \(y\in\{0,25\}\), and \(z=0,25,\ldots,100\), with the historical reference corner \((449,24,34)\) added explicitly. Of these 181 placements, 52 contain at least 97\% foreground in the T0--T1 union mask. A 5-voxel refinement is then performed over \(x=280,285,\ldots,380\), \(y=15,20,\ldots,35\), and \(z=40,45,\ldots,90\), again including the historical corner. This stage enumerates 1,156 placements and retains 831 after the same foreground filter.

For each retained corner \(\mathbf{c}\), the local T0--T1 displacement is estimated by axiswise roll agreement with parabolic sub-voxel refinement. After applying its rounded integer displacement, the fractional residual \(\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{c})\) is remeasured on the staggered pair. The six off-diagonal components of the local displacement-gradient tensor \(\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{c})\) are estimated by linear fits across five slabs. Candidates are ranked in ascending order by

\begin{equation}
s(\mathbf{c})
=
\left\|\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{c})\right\|_{\infty}
+
\frac{L-1}{2}
\max_i\sum_{j\ne i}\left|G_{ij}(\mathbf{c})\right|,
\qquad L=100.
\label{eq:window-selection-score}
\end{equation}

The two terms are expressed in voxels and enter with equal coefficient: the first is the largest post-stagger residual, and the second is the largest estimated half-window displacement ramp. Post-stagger agreement is retained as a diagnostic but does not enter the implemented ordering. The historical raw-agreement window ranks 831st under Equation~\eqref{eq:window-selection-score}, showing that raw agreement is insensitive to the residual and shear targeted by the search.

The score identifies a low-distortion family rather than a unique isolated optimum. The representative corner \((355,20,90)\) ranks sixth with \(s=0.823\), but its T0 crop contacts the specimen surface and contains approximately 0.96\% background. The production corner \((355,25,85)\) lies in the same quiet region, ranks 15th with \(s=0.855\), and reduces T0/T1 background to approximately 0.05\%/0.09\%. It was therefore selected by a documented secondary screen---near-optimal residual-driven score without the visible surface contact of the higher-ranked representative---rather than presented as the unconstrained score minimum. Subsequent three-initialization training gave the two corners comparable drift ranges, supporting the stability of this within-family choice.

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\delta}(\mathbf{x})
=
\operatorname{round}
\left[
\boldsymbol{\phi}
+\mathbf{G}\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{c}\right)
\right],
\label{eq:integer-registration}
\end{equation}
where \(\boldsymbol{\phi}\) is the remeasured fractional offset, \(\mathbf{G}\) is the fitted displacement-gradient tensor, and \(\mathbf{c}\) is the window center. Componentwise rounding copies existing categorical labels rather than synthesizing interpolated grain identities. Grain identities are linked between full-volume frames before registration using greedy one-to-one maximum-overlap matching. For T0--T1, 90.4\% of T1 grains and 97.4\% of T1 voxels inherit T0 identities; the remaining 2.6\% of T1 voxels generate all-zero target maps instead of fabricated identity assignments~\cite{tian2026experiment}.

The unmodified 3D-PRIMME architecture is then trained on T0--T1. A \(9^3\) observation and \(9^3\) action neighborhood yield 729 input/output features per boundary-centered sample. The training pair supplies 797,712 local samples, split randomly 80/20 for optimization monitoring, and three models are trained from different initializations. The experiment-trained model uses the same representation, target, and loss as Equations~\eqref{eq:primme-representation}--\eqref{eq:primme-loss}; only the source of \(S_i^{(t)}\) and \(y_{ij}^{(t)}\) changes from MF simulation to curated measurement~\cite{tian2026experiment}.

As an exploratory secondary diagnostic, effective-time reparameterization will be examined while physical-time and matched-state results remain primary. For a monotone marker \(z\), such as grain count or \(\langle R\rangle^2\), rollout step \(k\) is assigned an effective experimental time

\begin{equation}
t_{\mathrm{eff}}(k;z)
=
\underset{t\in[t_0,t_4]}{\operatorname{arg\,min}}
\left|
z_E(t)-z_{\hat E}(k)
\right|,
\qquad
z\in\left\{N_g,\langle R\rangle^2\right\},
\label{eq:effective-time}
\end{equation}
using monotone interpolations of the five experimental states. Because the matched grain count result places T2--T4 near rollout step 2, only approximately \(k=0,1,2\) fall distinctly within the experimental marker range. Effective time is therefore an exploratory clock diagnostic, not a calibrated trajectory. Piecewise-linear and monotone shape-preserving interpolation will be compared, and both grain count and \(\langle R\rangle^2\) must support the same qualitative conclusion. A pointwise mapping will be summarized only when at least three distinct in-range rollout steps, including an interior step, are supported; a clock-drift trend requires at least four distinct steps with at least two interior points. Interpolation sensitivity is acceptable only when both schemes place every supported rollout step in the same adjacent experimental interval and preserve the same qualitative conclusion for both markers; subinterval differences are not treated as resolved. If these conditions fail, support is insufficient, or the markers disagree, only raw-time and matched-state results will be retained.

Final reporting will give each metric family a supported, mixed, unsupported, or uninformative interpretation together with its simple baseline, model-replicate spread, and relevant readout sensitivity. A family is supported when improvement over its prespecified comparator is directionally consistent across all three model replicates and the substantive conclusion is not reversed by the relevant readout or sensitivity checks. It is mixed when improvement or stability holds only for a subset of states, replicates, quantities within the family, or analysis choices; unsupported when the prespecified comparison shows no replicate-stable improvement or a favorable conclusion is reversed by the relevant sensitivity check; and uninformative when temporal support, linkage, or measurement resolution is insufficient to evaluate the comparison. These labels summarize retrospective evidence; they are not confirmatory pass/fail decisions with a prospective error rate. Only supported outputs advance to experimental interpretation in Aim 2; mixed, unsupported, and uninformative outputs remain in the record.

The temporal-evaluation stage uses the curated T0--T4 trajectory, the three experiment-trained model replicates, and the existing rollout, linkage, topology, and distribution-analysis code. Aim 1 requires no new acquisition, second spatial window, or simulation initialized from the experimental state.

\subsection{Evaluation criteria, expected outcomes, and contingencies}

Aim 1 separates its preprocessing gate from its post-training diagnostics. The quantitative registration gate is mean T0--T1 label displacement no larger than 0.05 voxel on each axis; the curated pair satisfies this gate. Unmatched boundary fraction, linkage yield, retained volume, and comparison with the uncurated labels are reported to expose the remaining data limitations, but the existing analysis record does not assign them additional pass/fail thresholds. Directional rollout drift across the three trained models and grain-count monotonicity are post hoc diagnostics, not curation acceptance gates, because they depend on trained-model behavior or later measured states. Sensitivity conclusions must be reported across model replicates and coherent units rather than pooled voxel patches.

Aim 1 succeeds as an evaluation if all five metric families are reported against the comparisons in Table~\ref{tab:aim1-reliability-matrix}, uncertainty is based on model replicates and coherent units rather than pooled voxels, and the already-observed status of T2--T4 is stated explicitly. The descriptive rules above determine the final classification; no metric-specific cutoff will be selected post hoc from the observed T2--T4 results.

The achieved foundation is a curated single-window trajectory and a directly trained local operator whose label and field integrity are documented. The expected final result is a mixed fidelity profile: field morphology, early kinetics, and normalized size distributions are likely to add information beyond their simple baselines, while topology or individual-grain response may remain unsupported. That profile defines which outputs may be interpreted in Aim 2 within the selected trajectory.

The completed search record makes the selection reproducible while preserving its claim boundary: rank 15 is a near-optimal member of a low-distortion family chosen after a separate surface-contact screen, not the unconstrained minimizer of a single objective. If a future dataset fails the 0.05-voxel registration gate, direct-learning claims based on that pair will be withheld. If linkage is inadequate, identity-agnostic population or boundary quantities will be reported instead of grain-level response even when field and population diagnostics remain reportable. If T2--T4 do not support a stable effective-time mapping, no clock conclusion will be reported. No failed family will be replaced by a more favorable post hoc metric, and none of these analyses will be described as independent validation. Aim 1 does not establish spatial reproducibility elsewhere in the reconstruction.


\section{Aim 2: Interpretability and physics learning from experimentally learned evolution rules}

\subsection{Question and hypothesis}

Which spatial information does a reliable experiment-trained operator use, how is that information transformed into local evolution decisions, and which reproducible response signatures can be related to measurable grain-boundary geometry?

Experiment-trained operators that pass the Aim 1 field-integrity and temporal-evaluation gates will exhibit a reproducible hierarchy of spatial information and a localized, symmetry-consistent response structure. Short-range neighborhoods will carry the dominant transition information, while additional scales will improve trajectory-level fidelity. After numerical and data-induced artifacts are controlled, the resulting scale dependence and output sensitivities will be associated with measurable curvature, topology, and inclination descriptors. Relationships that remain stable across model initializations, available experimental states, relevant geometric strata, and a controlled simulation test may support bounded physical attribution within the selected trajectory; otherwise, they will remain model-level interpretations.

\subsection{Rationale and preliminary evidence}

Prediction alone does not establish physical understanding. The neighborhood study shows that a local sampling rule can alter macroscopic anisotropy and pinning, and 3D-PRIMME shows that a learned operator can recover prescribed inclination dependence~\cite{tian2026neighborhood,tian2026threeDprimme}. These findings make the local neighborhood scientifically meaningful, but they do not reveal which parts of that neighborhood drive a trained model or how the learned representation produces a transition. Gradient-based attribution provides a principled way to connect outputs to inputs, but attribution maps require explicit fidelity checks because visually plausible explanations can be insensitive to the learned model or training labels~\cite{sundararajan2017attribution,adebayo2018sanity}. The wider interpretability literature reinforces the point from several directions: saliency maps can shift under input transformations that leave the prediction unchanged~\cite{kindermans2019unreliability}; several popular attribution methods perform no better than random feature ranking when scored by retraining after removal~\cite{hooker2019roar}; and an explanation of a black box is not the same object as an account of the mechanism~\cite{rudin2019stopexplaining}. These are the reasons Aim 2 pairs every attribution summary with a functional test. The aim interrogates the same operator from two complementary directions. Input-side analysis asks which spatial scales supply useful information. Output-side analysis asks how perturbations of that information change the model's transition field. The simulation-trained operator provides a controlled development environment; the primary experimental target is the operator whose field integrity and relevant temporal outputs are supported by Aim 1.

Three simulation-domain analyses establish the feasibility of stable model interpretation: one input-side probe that asks which spatial scales supply useful information, and two output-side probes that ask how the model converts that information into a transition, through confidence conditioned on a local curvature proxy and through the action field and full Jacobian. None of them satisfies the experimental reliability gates or completes Aim 2. Their role is to validate the probes and expose numerical failure modes before the same analyses are transferred to experiment-trained operators.

The input-side feasibility study compares multiscale 3D-PRIMME models with observation windows \(7^3\), \(9^3\), and \(11^3\) against matched single-scale models across ten training seeds (Figure~\ref{fig:aim2-input-prelim}, top). The multiscale model produced the lowest error in the simulated mean-squared grain-radius trajectory (\(11.76\pm1.93\), compared with \(13.39\)--\(22.19\) for the single-scale models) while matching the best voxel-level accuracy (\(0.842\pm0.015\)). Relative \(L_1\) norms of the learned scale-mixing blocks were examined in two wider configurations, spanning five and eight observation windows (Figure~\ref{fig:aim2-input-prelim}, bottom); both assigned greater weight to smaller windows, with diminishing contributions from larger and closely spaced ones. Increasing the prediction gap from one to four frames shifted weight slightly toward larger windows, but the change was less than three percentage points; that comparison is not among the configurations plotted here. These results support the feasibility of scale attribution while also showing that raw mixer weights must be paired with functional ablations before they are interpreted physically.

The first output-side probe conditions model confidence on a local curvature proxy. For boundary voxel \(i\), this proxy is the number of neighboring voxels whose grain identity differs from the center in a \(3\times3\times3\) neighborhood,
\[
c_i=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{3\times3\times3}(i)\setminus i}
\mathbb{I}\!\left[g_j\neq g_i\right],
\]
where \(g_i\) is the grain identity at the center voxel. The model response is summarized by the maximum predicted transition score, \(q_i=\max_m p_i(m)\), and by output entropy. When the analysis is restricted to neighborhoods containing exactly two grain identities, \(q_i\) decreases toward the locally flat-interface proxy \(c_i\approx13\) and increases again for more strongly concave or convex configurations. Entropy shows the complementary trend. Including or excluding the center action produces no visible change because \(99.97\%\) of sampled boundary voxels select a neighboring identity. Pooling two-grain boundaries with higher-order junctions, by contrast, obscures the U-shaped response; junction neighborhoods constitute approximately \(65\%\) of the boundary-voxel population in this test. The result supports a topology-conditioned association between model confidence and a discrete curvature proxy, not direct recovery of a continuum curvature law.

The second output-side probe examines the action field and full input--output Jacobian. The mean \(9^3\) action-likelihood field is compact, centered to within \(0.16\) voxel, and accurately summarized by a three-dimensional Gaussian with per-voxel fitting error below \(0.01\). A full Jacobian pipeline was then verified against an analytic symmetric function to machine precision. The analysis exposed a storage-axis bias introduced by the padding implementation; assigning each training sample a uniformly selected cubic-group transformation removed the systematic bias without changing validation loss. In the symmetry-controlled model, the aggregate sensitivity contains a center dip, a nearest-neighbor ring, and an approximately isotropic decay (Figures~\ref{fig:aim2-jacobian-render} and~\ref{fig:aim2-output-prelim}), while each directional output preferentially responds to the input in its own direction.

Let \(S(r)\) denote the shell mean of the voxelwise total sensitivity \(S(n)\) at distance \(r\) from the neighborhood center. This radial sensitivity is currently represented by a Shell-Gauss+\(C\) candidate model,
\[
S(r)=
A_{\mathrm{shell}}
\exp\!\left[-\frac{(r-r_0)^2}{2\sigma_{\mathrm{shell}}^2}\right]
+
A_{\mathrm{bg}}
\exp\!\left[-\frac{r^2}{2\sigma_{\mathrm{bg}}^2}\right]
+C.
\]
The fitted expression is
\[
S(r)=
0.032
\exp\!\left[-\frac{(r-0.98)^2}{2(0.67)^2}\right]
+
0.022
\exp\!\left[-\frac{r^2}{2(1.75)^2}\right]
+0.011.
\]
The first term describes a sensitivity shell centered at approximately one voxel, the second describes a broader short-range background, and \(C\) represents the nonzero long-range tail. The fit uses shell means over the radial domain while excluding the eight corner voxels at \(r\approx6.93\). Figure~\ref{fig:aim2-jacobian-render} keeps the distinction between the measured aggregate Jacobian and its radial approximation explicit: the former is computed directly from network derivatives, whereas the latter is a smooth post-hoc representation of that response. The fit is a candidate summary of the learned response, not an independently learned kernel, a finalized physical law, or proof that a unique physical mechanism has been recovered.

\subsection{Proposed methodology}

Aim 2A will determine which spatial scales drive the experimental outputs supported by Aim 1. For the experiment-trained model family established and evaluated in Aim 1, matched single-scale and multiscale configurations will be evaluated on those output families. Comparing or transferring scale conclusions across simulation- and experiment-trained models lies outside the core plan, since the two sources do not yet share an audited set of commensurate metrics. If \(W_s\) denotes the block of the scale-mixing layer associated with observation scale \(s\), its global parameter-based importance will be summarized as
\[
I_s=\frac{\lVert W_s\rVert_1}{\sum_{r\in\mathcal{S}}\lVert W_r\rVert_1}.
\]
Because parameter magnitude is not equivalent to functional importance, \(I_s\) will be tested against scale removal, scale masking, and matched single-scale performance. Removal-based importance is the standard way to convert a parameter-space indicator into a functional claim, and reporting it as a range over equally well-performing models, instead of for one fitted model, guards against reading a single optimization outcome as a property of the problem~\cite{fisher2019allmodels,lundberg2017shap}. Scale rankings and ablation effects will be estimated across model initializations and checked across the available experimental states and relevant boundary-geometry strata. Prediction-horizon perturbations will test whether longer temporal targets increase dependence on broader spatial context.

Aim 2B will determine how local information becomes an evolution decision. Let \(K_i(n)\) denote input feature \(n\) in local sample \(i\), and let \(p_i(m)\) denote the predicted transition score for output position \(m\). The full input--output Jacobian is
\[
J_i(m,n)=\frac{\partial p_i(m)}{\partial K_i(n)}.
\]
The ensemble absolute sensitivity and total sensitivity of input position \(n\) will be calculated as
\[
\overline{J}_{\mathrm{abs}}(m,n)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|J_i(m,n)\right|,
\qquad
S(n)=\sum_m\overline{J}_{\mathrm{abs}}(m,n).
\]
Signed means will be reported together with absolute sensitivity and the sign-agreement ratio
\[
R(m,n)=
\frac{\left|\sum_i J_i(m,n)\right|}
{\sum_i\left|J_i(m,n)\right|},
\]
so that cancellation is not mistaken for weak dependence. These maps will quantify the spatial range, symmetry, and directional routing of the learned response. Because a Jacobian is a local derivative, it characterizes the response only near the evaluated inputs; where the conclusions must hold across the input distribution, variance-based global sensitivity indices provide the complementary aggregate measure~\cite{sobol2001indices}. They will be complemented by action-likelihood maps and by confidence or entropy conditioned on curvature proxy, boundary topology, and, where measurable, boundary inclination. Two-grain boundaries and higher-order junctions will be analyzed separately.

Aim 2C will separate stable model response from physical attribution. The interpretation pipeline will first be reproduced on simulation-trained models, where isotropy and prescribed anisotropy provide controlled tests. Cubic rotations and reflections, axis permutations, padding controls, and model replicates will be used to identify implementation-induced asymmetry before experimental results are interpreted. The relevant symmetry is a property the operator should possess and the implementation can silently break, as the storage-axis bias found in the preliminary Jacobian work demonstrates; architectures that enforce group equivariance by construction offer the alternative to auditing it after the fact, at the cost of committing to the symmetry group in advance~\cite{cohen2016equivariant,weiler2019steerable}. The same summaries will then be applied to the experiment-trained model family. A response will advance from Level 3 model interpretation to bounded Level 4 physical attribution only if it is reproducible across model initializations, experimental states, and relevant geometric strata, is associated with a measurable microstructural descriptor, and survives a controlled simulation test. If topology is unsupported in Aim 1, topology-conditioned patterns may be reported as model behavior but will not support topology-related physical attribution. Because the present curvature result separates two-grain interfaces from higher-order junctions, that stratification must also remain stable to the Aim 1 readout, speckle, small-grain, and connectivity analyses; otherwise, both the curvature--confidence relationship and the junction comparison will remain exploratory Level 3 behavior. A Level 5 candidate-mechanism claim requires an additional falsifiable prediction or intervention; agreement between a sensitivity map and a familiar pattern is not sufficient.

The work will use the existing multiscale and Jacobian simulation studies, symmetry-controlled simulation checkpoints, the reliable experiment-trained checkpoints and held-out predictions from Aim 1, and local descriptors derived from registered grain-ID volumes. Required descriptors include neighborhood composition, the current curvature proxy, boundary topology, and boundary inclination when it can be estimated at adequate spatial resolution.

\subsection{Evaluation criteria, expected outcomes, and contingencies}

Interpretation summaries will be reported across model replicates with bootstrap or replicate-based uncertainty. Input-side conclusions require agreement between scale-mixer rankings and at least one functional ablation, while output-side conclusions must be stable to sample size, the choice between signed and absolute aggregation, cubic coordinate transformations, available experimental states, and relevant geometric strata. Map stability will be evaluated on shell- or direction-aggregated summaries using Spearman rank correlation and normalized \(L_1\) difference. Split-half bootstrap resampling of coherent grains or spatial blocks within each checkpoint defines a pooled reference reliability distribution over the relevant checkpoints: a comparison across model replicates or experimental states is stable only when its rank correlation is no lower than the fifth percentile and its normalized \(L_1\) difference is no higher than the 95th percentile of that distribution. Residual variation under cubic coordinate transformations must not exceed the observed between-replicate variation. A descriptor effect must retain the same sign across all applicable model replicates and available experimental states and have a 95\% bootstrap interval excluding zero in both the experimental analysis and the controlled simulation test. Failure of any applicable condition limits the result to exploratory model behavior. These criteria distinguish stable model interpretation from bounded physical attribution; a candidate-mechanism claim still requires a falsifiable prediction or intervention.

Aim 2 is expected to distinguish stable model properties from responses attributable to measurable interface geometry and from candidate explanations that remain short of established mechanisms. Its output will be a symmetry-audited, functionally tested account of the spatial scales and local responses used by reliable experiment-trained operators.

The principal risks are that parameter weights may not reflect functional importance, voxel-level sensitivities or geometric descriptors may be noisy, and numerical anisotropy may be comparable to the physical signal. Functional ablations will supersede unstable weight rankings, and sensitivity will be aggregated by shell, direction, boundary class, or grain when voxel-level maps are unreliable. If observational data cannot separate competing explanations, controlled simulation will define the claim ceiling; if physical attribution does not survive the reliability tests, the result will remain validated model interpretation rather than a mechanism claim.

\chapter{Expected Outcomes, Contributions, and Research Plan}

\section{Analytical and methodological deliverables}

The completed work establishes two linked results: local neighborhood design controls the effective inclination response encoded by stochastic simulation labels, and a fixed local three-dimensional operator can learn and scale those controlled rules. These results supply the physical and computational foundation for the proposed transition to experimental supervision. On that foundation, the proposed work adds the following completed analyses and prespecified deliverables. Each will be produced and reported whatever the results show:

\begin{itemize}
\item a documented single-window workflow for residual-driven selection, label-preserving registration, grain linkage, direct training, and directional drift auditing;
\item persistence, majority-class, unchanged-distribution, readout, and topology-sensitivity comparisons for retrospective evaluation on T2--T4;
\item a metric-by-metric fidelity profile that retains unsupported results and states the limits imposed by one selected field of view;
\item symmetry-audited multiscale and Jacobian interpretation methods transferred to the experimental operator only for outputs supported by Aim 1.
\end{itemize}

These outputs shift the central methodological question from whether a network can fit one favorable transition to which evidence is required before a measured or simulated rollout can be used scientifically.

\section{Conditional contributions and scientific impact}

If Aim 1 temporal outputs improve over their simple comparators and remain stable to the reported readout choices, they will support bounded fidelity claims for the selected trajectory. If Aim 2 responses reproduce across model initializations, experimental states, geometric strata, and controlled simulation tests, selected signatures advance from stable model interpretation to bounded physical attribution. Formal probabilistic calibration, spatial or material transfer, and a unique mechanism claim are not supported by the available single-window dataset.

For experimental materials science, the work provides a way to use sparse 4D grain maps without allowing registration or segmentation structure to masquerade as dynamics. For mesoscale modeling, it separates control of the simulated label source from fidelity of its surrogate and from fidelity to experiment. For scientific machine learning, it demonstrates a reusable evidence ladder linking field integrity, mesoscale prediction, model interpretation, and physical attribution. A curated dataset or software release would further broaden this impact, but public release will be promised only after data ownership and sharing permissions are confirmed.

\chapter{Conclusion}

A surrogate cannot be better than the process that generated its labels. The neighborhood study made that visible in simulation: what a training label contains is partly a property of the neighborhood that produced it. 3D-PRIMME then showed that such a label can be learned and scaled beyond the volume it was fitted on. The experimental work changes the label source from a model to a measurement, and with it the failure mode: a network trained on an uncurated 4D sequence learns acquisition motion rather than boundary motion.

The proposed research follows one experimental trajectory from selection through curation, training, evaluation, and interpretation. One trajectory cannot establish how grains grow in general. It can establish what has to be true of the data, the model, and the analysis before that question is worth asking of a learned operator at all.
